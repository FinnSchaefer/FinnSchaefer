PULSER at IEEE HOST 2026

PULSER is probably the clearest example of the kind of work I want to keep doing. At its core, it looks at what happens when a system keeps trusting sensor input that is subtly manipulated, and how that shifts the system's understanding of its environment over time.

What matters to me is not just the robotics side. It points to a broader issue. A lot of cyber-physical systems do not fail because something obviously breaks. They fail because they trust the wrong data. If the input still looks reasonable, bad decisions can propagate much further before anything raises a flag.

That is why I think PULSER matters beyond ROS2 or SLAM. It pushes on a more general question that shows up everywhere: what happens when a system depends on continuous sensor input, but the trust model around that input is wrong? This is about understanding and exploiting trust failures in systems that rely on continuous, believable data.

Why I keep pointing back to it

This paper is important to me because it ties together the parts of security work I care about most: cyber-physical systems, perception trust, and failures subtle enough to survive ordinary assumptions. It also sharpened how I want to frame future work, not just whether a system can be attacked, but how trusted data gets turned into decisions the system is willing to act on.

How it fits the rest of the site

PULSER sits at the center of the site because it connects the research side and the engineering side cleanly. It is a publication, but it also sets the logic for the rest of the work here: understand the trust boundary, understand what the system assumes, and then ask what happens when those assumptions stop being true.
